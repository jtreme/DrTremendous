Multicollinearity
ARE 455 · When OLS can't tell your variables apart
Multicollinearity means the model can't tell which variable deserves credit. What's going on? ›
The estimates bounce around even when overall model fit still looks strong. OLS asks what each X contributes holding the others fixed. There are two bins because there are two variables, but the signal separating them is weak. When X2 and X3 move together, you almost never observe X2 changing while X3 stays fixed. The model is trying to answer a question the data give very little information about. Standard errors explode and t-stats collapse, but overall model fit often stays strong.

Why are there two bins if there's only one signal? ›
Because the theory says there are two different effects. OLS builds a bin for each. But when X2 and X3 almost always move together, the data give very little guidance on how much belongs in each bin, so both estimates become unreliable.

Example: X2 = study hours, X3 = attendance. If students who study more almost always attend more, did grades improve because of studying, or because of attending? You need two bins conceptually. But the data give you mostly one combined signal.

When r is high: many different splits between the two bins explain the data almost equally well. That flat ridge of near-ties is the consequence of multicollinearity, and it's why the estimates swing so much from sample to sample.

"The ball didn't go to the wrong bin. The data just can't pin down which bin it belongs in."
What causes multicollinearity?
↑ Variables that are strongly correlated in the real world
Income and wealth. Years of experience and age. Square footage and number of rooms. They aren't the same thing, but they move together in most data, so OLS has very little independent variation to work with when separating their effects.
↑ Including a variable and its transformation
X and X², or X and log(X). When X is all positive and its range is narrow, these are often highly correlated in your sample, so their individual coefficients get noisy. Two standard fixes: center X before squaring, which usually cuts the correlation sharply, and test the variables jointly (F-test) rather than one t-stat at a time.
↑ Small sample with many predictors
Two problems compound here. Sample correlations between even unrelated variables bounce around more when n is small, so chance collinearity is more likely. And as the number of predictors approaches the number of observations, the data spreads thinner over more dimensions and every estimate gets noisier, collinear or not. More data helps both problems.
